Polarization in news consumption -- Republicans and Democrats selecting into different news sources -- is often interpreted as evidence for demand for like-minded ideological slant in reporting. I show that this interpretation is incomplete: Republicans and Democrats differ in their tastes not just for ideological rhetoric but also in topic coverage, even for topics that are not inherently political. I construct a measure of partisan slant that conditions on topic, isolating within-topic differences in politicized rhetoric from cross-party differences in issue emphasis. I use this measure to estimate a demand model in which Republican and Democratic consumers have distinct, party-specific tastes for partisan slant and topic coverage. I find that consumers in both parties prefer politicized rhetoric that favors their own ideological positions. However, while Democrats prefer topic selection that mirrors that of major national papers, Republicans prefer more specialized reporting. Omitting these differences in topic preferences meaningfully biases demand estimation, potentially mispredicting the ideological composition of readership by amounts exceeding the mean level of polarization. I then model how firms choose slant and topic coverage in a simultaneous-move game. Because firms may differentiate over both dimensions of reporting, policies aimed at limiting political slant in reporting are not sufficient to address polarization in news consumption. A counterfactual simulation of subsidized entry of local news outlets predicts a 17% increase in average polarized consumption, counter to the stated goals of ongoing policy proposals.
